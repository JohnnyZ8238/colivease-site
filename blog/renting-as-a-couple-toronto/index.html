Renting a Room as a Couple in Toronto: A Practical Guide

Renting a room as a couple in Toronto? How to search together, who can say no to two people, when a partner can move in and how to get added to a lease.

Updated November 21, 2026 · 7 min read · ColivEase

Renting a room as a couple in Toronto is very doable, but you'll have fewer listings to choose from and more questions to answer than a single renter. Be upfront that you're two people, focus on places where the space and the house actually suit a couple, and make sure both of you end up with real rights in writing, ideally as tenants on the lease.

This guide is for couples hunting for a place together, and for couples where one partner already rents and the other wants to join. If you're only wondering about overnight stays and how long a partner can visit, see our guide to guest rules for roommates.

Key takeaways

  • Say "two people" in your first message. Many room listings are priced and set up for one person.
  • Owner-occupied rooms play by different rules. A live-in owner you share a kitchen or bathroom with can generally say no to couples.
  • In most other rentals, refusing you because you're unmarried can raise a Human Rights Code issue.
  • Space and safety limits are real. A landlord can act if too many people live in a unit on a continuing basis and that breaks health, safety or housing standards.
  • Get both names on the lease, through a new lease, a written addition, or an assignment when a roommate leaves.
  • Plan for a breakup before you sign, not after.

This is general information, not legal advice. For your situation, contact the Landlord and Tenant Board, a community legal clinic, or the Human Rights Legal Support Centre.

Why live-in owners are different

Under section 5(i) of the Residential Tenancies Act (RTA), the Act generally doesn't apply when you must share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner, or certain close family members, who live in the building. The Human Rights Code has a similar exemption for that kind of shared home in section 21(1). In plain terms, a live-in owner has a lot of freedom to decide who shares their kitchen, and "single occupant only" is their call. Our guide to living with your landlord explains what you give up in that setup.

What most other landlords can't do

Outside that exemption, the Ontario Human Rights Code protects people from discrimination in housing based on grounds that include marital status and family status. The Ontario Human Rights Commission (OHRC) explains that marital status covers people living together in a conjugal relationship outside marriage, so "we only rent to married couples" is a red flag.

That's different from a landlord who says a small room genuinely can't fit two people, or that adding a person would break occupancy standards. Limits tied to real space, health or safety rules aren't the same as refusing you for who you are. If you think you've been turned away for a protected reason, the Human Rights Legal Support Centre offers free help.

How to search as a couple

Write a joint profile

Landlords and roommates want to know who's moving in. Cover:

  • both names, jobs or programs, and schedules
  • your combined budget and how you'll pay (one e-transfer or two)
  • how often friends visit, and whether either of you works from home
  • that you'll both sign the lease and both be screened

Questions to ask before you view

  • Is the room priced for one or two people, and are utilities affected?
  • Does the host live in the home, and do we share a kitchen or bathroom with them?
  • Will both of us be named on the lease?
  • How do the current roommates feel about a couple?

Couples in shared houses: keep it fair

Two people use more hot water, fridge space and bathroom time than one. Agreeing on a fair split up front, often a bit more than a single share, keeps roommates from resenting you. Our guide to splitting rent fairly has formulas you can show the house.

Can my partner move in?

If you already rent and your partner wants to join you, start with three questions:

  1. Who is your landlord? If you share a kitchen or bathroom with a live-in owner, your written agreement and the owner's wishes rule. Ask first.
  2. What does your lease say? Read any additional terms about occupants or utilities.
  3. Is there space? A landlord can serve a notice (Form N5) if the number of people living in a unit on a continuing basis breaks health, safety or housing standards.

Even where you don't strictly need permission, telling your landlord and roommates is the smart move. A partner who quietly moves in is how roommate disputes start. The guest rules guide linked above covers the line between a frequent visitor and someone who now lives there.

Adding someone to a lease in Ontario

If your partner just lives with you without being on the lease, they're usually an occupant. LTB Interpretation Guideline 21 explains that occupants generally can't bring applications at the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB), and must usually leave if the tenancy ends. Getting them added fixes that.

On assignment, remember that once a landlord agrees in principle, they can't arbitrarily or unreasonably refuse the specific person you propose.

Watch for rent surprises

If your landlord wants to raise the rent because your partner moved in, pause. In a tenancy covered by the RTA, increases are limited by the annual guideline and timing rules. Ask a community legal clinic before agreeing to a surcharge in writing.

Plan for the "what if"

Nobody signs a lease expecting a breakup, but a short written note between you saves a lot of pain:

  • who keeps the place if you split
  • how much notice the other person gives
  • how the deposit and any furniture are divided
  • how the rent is covered until the leaving partner is off the lease

Frequently asked questions

Should a couple apply as one application or two?

Most landlords want to screen every adult who will live in the unit, so expect both of you to share ID, income or school details and references. Presenting yourselves as one household with one combined budget makes the decision easier. On ColivEase you can apply as a roommate team so the landlord sees you together.

Why do room listings charge more for a couple?

Two people use more utilities, hot water and shared space, so many hosts quote a higher price for a couple when the room is first rented. That starting price is negotiable, so ask what exactly the extra covers. Raising rent later, once you are a tenant, is a different matter and is limited by the Residential Tenancies Act.

Do both of us need to be on the lease?

It isn't required, but it's the strongest protection for the partner who would otherwise be an occupant. A tenant on the lease has rights at the Landlord and Tenant Board; an occupant generally doesn't and usually has to leave if the tenant's tenancy ends.

Is a couple's room cheaper than renting a one-bedroom?

Often, yes, because you share a kitchen, bathroom and utilities with others. The trade-off is less privacy and more house rules. Compare the total monthly cost, including utilities and internet, against what you'd each pay for separate rooms or a small apartment.

What happens to the lease if we break up?

If you're both on the lease, one partner's N9 generally doesn't end the tenancy for the other, and you both may stay responsible for rent until it's sorted out. Agree early, in writing, on who would stay, how much notice the other gives and how the deposit would be split.
