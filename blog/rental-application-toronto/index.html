Rental Application Toronto: Documents and Tips to Win

Win your rental application in Toronto: documents to prepare, what landlords can and can't ask under the Human Rights Code, group tips and a strong intro.

Updated October 31, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

To win a rental application in Toronto, have a complete package ready before you view: photo ID, a recent credit report from Equifax or TransUnion, an employment letter, recent pay stubs and references, plus a short, friendly intro about who you are. Apply quickly, answer questions honestly, and know your rights: under Ontario's Human Rights Code, a landlord can't turn you down because of things like family status or receiving public assistance.

Key takeaways

  • Prepare your documents in advance as clear PDFs, so you can apply the same day you view.
  • Pull your own credit report for free from Equifax or TransUnion, and fix errors early.
  • No credit history? Offer a guarantor, proof of funds, a larger reference list or proof of enrolment.
  • Landlords can ask for rental history, credit references, a credit check, income information (alongside those) and a guarantor.
  • Landlords can't refuse you based on Human Rights Code grounds, such as family status, receipt of public assistance, race, place of origin, citizenship or disability.
  • Applying as a group? Submit one tidy package with everyone's documents and a single intro.

This is general information, not legal advice. For your situation, contact the Landlord and Tenant Board or a community legal clinic. For discrimination concerns, the Human Rights Legal Support Centre can help.

What landlords can and can't ask

Ontario has clear rules about tenant screening. The Ontario Human Rights Code protects you from discrimination in housing, and a regulation under the Code (O. Reg. 290/98) sets out the screening practices landlords can use.

What landlords can ask for

  • Rental history and landlord references
  • Credit references and permission to run a credit check
  • Income information, but the Ontario Human Rights Commission (OHRC) says it should be considered together with rental history, credit references and credit checks, not on its own
  • A guarantor

The OHRC has also said that rigid rent-to-income cut-offs (for example, "rent must be under 30% of income") on their own can be discriminatory, and that a lack of rental or credit history shouldn't be held against you.

What landlords can't refuse you for

Under the Human Rights Code, landlords can't treat you differently because of:

  • race, colour, ancestry, place of origin, ethnic origin or citizenship
  • creed (religion)
  • sex, sexual orientation, gender identity or gender expression
  • age
  • marital status or family status (for example, having children or being pregnant)
  • disability
  • receipt of public assistance (such as Ontario Works or ODSP)

The exception: these protections generally don't apply if you'd share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's family who live there. Read more in our guide to living with your landlord in Ontario.

Other common questions

  • SIN: You don't have to provide your Social Insurance Number. The federal Privacy Commissioner says it isn't required for a credit check.
  • Deposits: For tenancies covered by the Residential Tenancies Act, the landlord can collect a rent deposit of no more than one month's rent, plus a refundable key deposit. Damage deposits aren't allowed.
  • Post-dated cheques: A landlord can't require them, or require automatic withdrawals from your bank account.
  • Months of prepaid rent: You can choose to offer it, but a landlord can't demand it, and you should be careful about handing over large sums before signing.

Applying with no Canadian credit history

Newcomers and international students often have strong finances but no Canadian credit file. Fill the gap with:

  • a guarantor in Canada with good credit,
  • bank statements or a letter showing funds for your study period,
  • your study permit and enrolment letter, or a job offer letter,
  • a reference from a landlord back home, translated if needed.

Applying as a group

Many Toronto renters apply together to afford a larger place. Landlords like groups that look organized.

  1. Agree on the basics first: budget, move-in date, lease length and how rent will be split. Our guide to splitting rent fairly helps.
  2. Make one shared folder with everyone's documents, named consistently.
  3. Write one intro that covers each person in a sentence or two.
  4. Pick a point person to handle messages, so the landlord isn't juggling four email threads.
  5. Decide in advance what happens if one person drops out.

How to write a strong rental intro

Keep it short, warm and specific. Landlords read a lot of these.

Include:

  • who you are (job or program, and how long you've been in Toronto or Canada)
  • why this place fits (close to work or campus, the right size)
  • your move-in date and how long you hope to stay
  • one or two reliability signals (stable job, references, quiet lifestyle, non-smoker)
  • a thank-you and how to reach you

Example:

Hi Maria, thank you for showing us the unit on Saturday. We're two friends: Wei is a second-year engineering student at the University of Toronto, and Sam is a full-time analyst downtown. We'd love a 12-month lease starting December 1, and the walk to campus and the office is perfect for us. We're both non-smokers, quiet on weeknights and have attached our credit reports, employment and enrolment letters, and references. Happy to answer any questions. Thanks for considering us!

Red flags while you're applying

Most landlords are legitimate, but rental applications collect a lot of personal information, so stay alert:

  • Money before a viewing. Never send a deposit for a place you (or someone you trust) haven't seen in person or on a live video call.
  • Pressure to decide immediately with no lease to review, or a landlord who refuses to put anything in writing.
  • Requests for unusual documents, like your SIN, your bank login, or photos of your passport sent over a messaging app with no explanation.
  • A rent that seems far below similar listings in the same neighbourhood.
  • Payment by gift card, crypto or wire transfer to an overseas account.
  • A landlord who can't show they own or manage the property.

Before sending documents, it's fair to ask who will see them and how long they'll be kept. Share only what's needed, and watermark copies with "For rental application at [address] only."

Frequently asked questions

What documents do I need for a rental application in Toronto?

Most landlords ask for photo ID, a recent credit report from Equifax or TransUnion, an employment letter, recent pay stubs and references from past landlords or employers. Students often add proof of enrolment and funding, and many applicants offer a guarantor. Having everything ready as PDFs lets you apply the same day you view a place.

Do I have to give my landlord my SIN?

No. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada says a SIN isn't required for a credit check and providing it should be optional. You can offer your name, date of birth and address, or pull your own credit report and share it.

Can a landlord refuse me because I have kids or receive social assistance?

No. Under the Ontario Human Rights Code, landlords can't refuse to rent to you because of family status, such as having children, or because you receive public assistance like Ontario Works or ODSP. There's a narrow exception when you would share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's family.

Can a landlord ask for more than one month's rent up front in Ontario?

For tenancies covered by the RTA, a landlord can only require a rent deposit of up to one month's rent (or one rental period, if less), plus a refundable key deposit. They also can't require post-dated cheques or automatic withdrawals. Some tenants choose to offer prepaid rent, but a landlord can't demand it.

Can a landlord ask for a guarantor?

Yes. Ontario's rules allow landlords to ask for a guarantor, along with credit references, rental history and a credit check. A guarantor is often helpful for students and newcomers without Canadian credit history.
