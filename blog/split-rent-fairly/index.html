How to Split Rent With Roommates Fairly (With Example)

How to split rent with roommates fairly: equal, by room size or hybrid, with private bathroom and parking premiums, utilities and a worked example in CAD.

Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

The fairest way to split rent with roommates depends on how equal the rooms are. If the bedrooms are similar, split the rent equally. If they're not, use a hybrid: share part of the rent equally for the common areas, divide the rest by bedroom size, and add agreed premiums for extras like a private bathroom or a parking spot. Split utilities and internet per person, and write the numbers into a roommate agreement.

Below you'll find the main methods, how to price extras, a step-by-step worked example in Canadian dollars, and how to handle bills and rent increases.

Key takeaways

  • Equal rooms, equal rent. Don't overcomplicate it if the rooms are similar.
  • Unequal rooms? Use a hybrid. Shared space split equally, private space split by size.
  • Price the extras separately. Ensuite bathrooms, parking, balconies and big closets get a flat premium.
  • Couples count as two for shared space and utilities.
  • Agree before signing the lease, and put the numbers in writing.

Method 1: Equal split

Divide the total rent by the number of roommates.

  • Best for: rooms of similar size and quality, short stays, friends who value simplicity.
  • Watch for: resentment if one room is clearly better. If someone says "it's fine" a little too quickly, check again.

Method 2: By bedroom size

Measure each bedroom (in square feet or square metres), add them up, and give each person a share of the rent equal to their room's share of the total bedroom area.

  • Best for: rooms that differ mainly in size.
  • Watch for: this can overcharge the big room, because everyone uses the kitchen, living room and bathrooms equally. That's why most people prefer the hybrid below.

Method 3: Hybrid (shared + private)

This is the method most households land on.

  1. Decide what portion of the rent pays for shared space (kitchen, living room, hallways). Many people use 40 to 60 percent; you can also estimate it from the floor plan.
  2. Split that shared portion equally.
  3. Split the remaining private portion by bedroom size.
  4. Add premiums for extras (see below).
  • Best for: most apartments and houses with uneven rooms.
  • Watch for: agree on the shared-space percentage before you calculate, so no one picks the number that suits them.

Method 4: Feature-based adjustments

Some households start from an equal split and then adjust for features, one by one. It's less precise but easy to discuss.

Method 5: Bidding

Each roommate privately writes down what they'd pay for each room, and rooms go to the people who value them most, with prices adjusted so the total matches the rent. Online "fair rent division" calculators use versions of this idea. It's useful when people strongly prefer different rooms, but it can feel complicated, so make sure everyone understands the rules first.

How to price the extras

There's no official formula for these. A good way to set a premium is to compare listings in your area: how much more do similar rooms with that feature tend to cost? Then agree on a round number together.

Worked example (CAD)

Here's a hypothetical three-bedroom apartment. The rent and room sizes are made up for illustration, so plug in your own numbers. Toronto rents vary widely by neighbourhood and building.

The home

  • Total rent: $3,300 per month, including one parking spot
  • Room A: 14 m², with ensuite bathroom
  • Room B: 11 m², uses the parking spot
  • Room C: 9 m²

Step 1: Take out the parking. The roommates agree parking is worth $100. B pays it. Remaining rent: $3,200.

Step 2: Take out the ensuite premium. They agree on $150. A pays it. Remaining rent: $3,050.

Step 3: Split the rest 50/50 into shared and private.

  • Shared portion: $1,525, split equally = $508.33 each
  • Private portion: $1,525, split by bedroom area (total 34 m²)
    • A: 14 ÷ 34 × $1,525 = $627.94
    • B: 11 ÷ 34 × $1,525 = $493.38
    • C: 9 ÷ 34 × $1,525 = $403.68

Step 4: Add it up.

RoommateSharedPrivateExtrasTotal (rounded)
A$508.33$627.94$150 ensuite$1,286
B$508.33$493.38$100 parking$1,102
C$508.33$403.68none$912
Total$3,300

For comparison, an equal split would be $1,100 each. With this method, C pays less for the smallest room with no extras, and A pays more for the largest room and a private bathroom. Round to whole dollars and make sure the total still equals the rent.

Splitting utilities and internet

Usage is hard to measure per person, so most households split bills equally by head, not by room.

  • Example: hydro $90 + internet $75 = $165 per month, split three ways = $55 each.
  • Couple in one room? Count them as two people for utilities.
  • Heavy users: if someone works from home every day with a space heater or a gaming PC, agree on a small monthly top-up instead of tracking every kilowatt-hour.
  • Whose name? Put each account in one person's name, post the bill in your group chat, and pay within a set number of days.
  • Included in rent? Check your lease to see which utilities the landlord covers before you set up any accounts.

Special situations

Real households are messier than a spreadsheet. Here's how to handle the usual curveballs.

Someone moves in or out mid-month

Prorate by days. Take that person's monthly share, divide by the number of days in the month, and multiply by the days they actually live there. For example, a $912 share in a 30-day month is about $30.40 a day, so moving in on the 16th (15 days) works out to roughly $456. Agree on who covers the gap if a room sits empty between roommates.

Furnished vs. unfurnished rooms

If one room comes with a bed, desk and dresser and the others don't, a small premium is reasonable. If one roommate supplied most of the shared furniture, some households give them a small discount instead, or agree that the furniture leaves with them.

Different incomes

Some friends and couples prefer to split by income rather than by room, for example 60/40. That's a personal choice and can work well between partners, but between roommates it tends to cause friction over time. If you go this way, write down the percentages and when you'll review them.

One person does all the admin

The roommate who collects rent, pays the landlord and manages bills is doing real work. Rather than adjusting their rent, many households rotate the job each lease term, or share the bill accounts so no one person carries all the risk.

When the rent goes up

If your landlord raises the rent, apply the same percentage increase to everyone's share so the split stays fair. In Ontario, most units that were occupied on or before November 15, 2018 are subject to the annual rent increase guideline, which is 2.1% for 2026, and landlords generally must give at least 90 days' written notice and can raise rent only once every 12 months. Our Ontario rent increase guideline 2026 article has the details.

Tips to avoid money fights

  • Agree before anyone signs the lease. It's much harder to renegotiate after move-in.
  • Show your maths. Share the spreadsheet so everyone can check it.
  • Pay one person or the landlord directly by a set date each month, by e-transfer with a note.
  • Revisit when things change: someone moves out, a partner moves in, or rent increases.
  • Write it down in a roommate agreement so the numbers aren't up for debate later.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fairest way to split rent with roommates?

When rooms are similar, an equal split is simplest and usually fair. When they differ, a hybrid works well: split part of the rent equally for shared spaces, split the rest by bedroom size, then add agreed premiums for extras like a private bathroom or parking.

How much more should the person with a private bathroom pay?

There's no fixed rule. Many households agree on a flat monthly premium or a percentage, based on how much more similar rooms with an ensuite cost locally. Agree on the number together before signing so no one feels surprised later.

Should a couple sharing one room pay more?

Usually, yes. They take one bedroom but use twice the shared space, hot water and electricity. A common approach is to split the shared-space portion and utilities per person rather than per room.

How should roommates split utilities and internet?

Most households split utilities and internet equally per person, since usage is hard to measure. If one person works from home full time or runs heavy equipment, you can agree on a small adjustment.

What happens to our rent split if the rent goes up?

Apply the increase proportionally, so each person's share rises by the same percentage. In Ontario, most rent-controlled units are limited to the annual rent increase guideline, which is 2.1% for 2026, and landlords must give proper written notice.
