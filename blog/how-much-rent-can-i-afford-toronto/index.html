How Much Rent Can I Afford in Toronto? (2026)

How much rent can I afford in Toronto? Use the 30% rule as a starting point, budget for the extras, see how roommates change the math, and know your rights.

Updated November 21, 2026 · 7 min read · ColivEase

How much rent can you afford in Toronto? A common starting point is to keep rent at or below about 30% of your before-tax income, which means multiplying your monthly rent by 40 to see the yearly salary it implies. Then check the result against your take-home pay and your other costs, because utilities, internet, insurance and transit can add a lot. If the numbers don't work for a place on your own, sharing with roommates is the most reliable way to bring rent into range.

This guide turns that rule of thumb into a real monthly budget, shows how roommates change the math, and explains what Ontario landlords can and can't do with your income information.

Key takeaways

  • 30% is a guide, not a law. It's useful for planning, but your fixed costs, debts and savings goals matter just as much.
  • Quick math: monthly rent × 40 = the yearly before-tax income the 30% rule suggests.
  • Budget for the extras. Rent is only the biggest line; utilities, internet, tenant insurance and transit come on top.
  • Roommates change everything. A room or a shared two-bedroom can cut your housing cost dramatically.
  • "3x rent" isn't the whole story in Ontario. Landlords may consider income, but not as a rigid cut-off on its own.

How much rent can I afford in Toronto?

Start with the simple version, then refine it.

Step 1: The 30 percent rule for rent

Take your before-tax annual income, multiply by 0.30, and divide by 12. That's the monthly housing cost the rule suggests.

  • $48,000 a year → about $1,200 a month
  • $60,000 a year → about $1,500 a month
  • $80,000 a year → about $2,000 a month

CMHC uses the same 30% line when it defines affordable housing, which is why you see it everywhere. It works best as a ceiling for planning, not a target to spend up to.

Step 2: Check it against take-home pay

You pay rent from what actually lands in your account. After income tax, CPP and EI deductions, your take-home pay is noticeably lower than your gross salary. If rent would take close to half of your take-home pay, the rest of your month will feel tight, especially in winter when heating bills rise in some homes.

How roommates change affordability

Sharing doesn't just split the rent; it splits many of the extras too.

  • A room in a shared home is the cheapest way in, often with utilities and internet included.
  • A shared two-bedroom gives each person a private room plus a living room, often at a per-person cost close to a single room.
  • Renting a whole house as a group, room by room can work well for friends or matched roommates, with each person paying for their own room.

A few cautions:

  • If you're co-tenants on one lease, each of you is generally responsible to the landlord for the full rent, not just your share. Choose people whose finances you trust.
  • Unequal rooms should cost different amounts. Our guide to how to split rent with roommates walks through fair methods.
  • Put the split, the bills and what happens if someone leaves in a written roommate agreement.

Rent to income ratio in Ontario: what landlords can ask

Many landlords informally look for rent at about a third of monthly income, often phrased as "income of 3x rent." Ontario's rules are more nuanced.

Can a landlord ask for 3x rent income?

A landlord can ask about income. Under Ontario's Human Rights Code and its regulation on landlord business practices (O. Reg. 290/98), landlords may ask for rental history, credit references and credit checks, and may consider income information together with those details. According to the Ontario Human Rights Commission:

  • Income information should be used to confirm you have enough income to pay the rent, not as a strict cut-off.
  • Applying a rigid rent-to-income ratio, such as a 30% cut-off, is illegal unless you're applying for subsidized (rent-geared-to-income) housing.
  • Income can be considered on its own only when no other information, such as rental history or credit references, is available.
  • A lack of rental or credit history should not count against you, which matters for newcomers and students.

Ontario courts have upheld a finding that refusing applicants solely on rent-to-income ratios or minimum income criteria was discriminatory.

How to show you can pay without a "3x" income

  • A letter of employment or job offer
  • Proof of savings or funds, such as bank statements or a student's proof of funds
  • References from previous landlords, in Canada or abroad
  • Applying with roommates, so the landlord sees combined ability to pay

Our guide to renting with no credit history in Canada covers these documents in detail.

This is general information, not legal advice. If you believe you were refused housing because of a rigid income rule or the source of your income, contact the Human Rights Legal Support Centre or a community legal clinic.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 30 percent rule for rent?

It's a rule of thumb that housing costs should take no more than about 30% of your before-tax income. CMHC uses the same 30% line to define affordable housing. It's a planning guide, not a legal limit, and many Toronto renters spend more, which is one reason sharing is so common.

What salary do I need to rent in Toronto?

Using the 30% rule, multiply the monthly rent by 40 to get the yearly before-tax income it suggests. For the City of Toronto average unfurnished one-bedroom asking rent of $1,969 in liv.rent's October 2026 report, that works out to roughly $79,000 a year. A shared room costs far less, so the income it implies is much lower too.

Can a landlord refuse me because my income comes from savings or social assistance?

Not simply for that reason. The Ontario Human Rights Commission says a landlord can't turn someone away just because their income comes from a source other than a job, such as savings, a pension or social assistance, and receiving public assistance is a protected ground in housing. Landlords can still check that you can afford the rent.

How do I budget if my income is irregular?

Base your rent on your lower-earning months, not your best ones, and keep a buffer of at least a month or two of rent in savings. When you apply, bank statements covering several months, contracts or invoices help show a landlord your average income.

Should I use gross or take-home pay for my rent budget?

The 30% rule is usually quoted on before-tax (gross) income, but you pay rent from take-home pay. Check your budget against your net pay as well: if rent takes close to half of what lands in your account, the rest of your budget will feel very tight.
