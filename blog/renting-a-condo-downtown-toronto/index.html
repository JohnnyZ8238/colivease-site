Renting a Condo in Downtown Toronto: Tenant Guide

Renting a condo in downtown Toronto? Learn how condo rules bind tenants, how to book the elevator to move in, and what to ask about fobs, fees and amenities.

Updated November 21, 2026 · 7 min read · ColivEase

Renting a condo in downtown Toronto means you have two sets of rules: your lease with the owner, and the condo corporation's declaration, by-laws and rules, which bind you as a resident. In practice that affects how you move in (elevator bookings and deposits), how you get in (fobs), what you can do (amenities, pets, short-term rentals) and who you call when something breaks. Get the building's rules and move-in procedure before you sign, and you avoid most of the surprises.

This guide walks through how condo rentals work for tenants, the questions worth asking, and a move-in checklist you can actually use.

Key takeaways

  • Your landlord is the owner, not the building. Rent, repairs inside the unit and your lease are between you and the owner.
  • Condo rules still bind you. The Condominium Act requires residents, including tenants, to follow the declaration, by-laws and rules.
  • Book the elevator early. Move-ins usually need a reserved slot and often a refundable deposit paid to the corporation.
  • Condo fees are the owner's cost. Ask which utilities you pay on top of rent.
  • Short-term rentals are tightly limited. Toronto requires principal residence and City registration, and many condos ban them.

How renting a condo in downtown Toronto works

When you rent a condo unit, you sign a lease with the unit owner (or their agent). Most residential tenancies in Ontario use the standard lease, and the Residential Tenancies Act applies to your relationship with the owner as it does in any other rental.

The condominium corporation is a separate party. It runs the building through a board and usually a property management company, and it controls the common elements: lobby, elevators, hallways, gym, party room and parking garage. You don't pay the corporation rent, and it isn't your landlord, but its rules apply to you.

The Condominium Authority of Ontario says landlords must notify the corporation in writing when they lease a unit and provide a copy of the lease within 10 days of signing. That's one reason the management office may ask you to fill in a resident information form when you arrive.

Condo rules for tenants

Condo governing documents come in three layers. If they conflict, the Act comes first, then the declaration, then the by-laws, then the rules.

  • Declaration: the condo's "constitution." It often covers big-ticket restrictions such as pets, use of units and sometimes short-term rentals.
  • By-laws: how the corporation is run.
  • Rules: day-to-day life: move-ins, garbage and recycling, balcony use, barbecues, noise, amenity bookings and guest parking.

Under the Act, everyone living in the building must comply, and owners must take reasonable steps to make sure their tenants do. If you break a rule, the corporation can pursue the owner, and some disputes about noise, nuisance, pets, parking and storage can go to the Condominium Authority Tribunal. Your lease may also treat a rule breach as a problem between you and your landlord.

Ask for these before you sign: the current rules, the declaration's sections on pets and leasing, and the move-in procedure. A good landlord or agent will send them without fuss.

Amenities, guests and pets

  • Amenities: gyms, pools and party rooms usually have hours, booking systems and sometimes a refundable booking deposit. Some buildings require a resident fob to enter.
  • Guests: many buildings require guests to be registered with concierge, and visitor parking is often limited by time.
  • Pets: a "no pets" clause in an Ontario lease is generally void, but a condo declaration can still restrict pets. If you have a pet, check before signing. Our guide to renting with pets in Toronto goes deeper.

Booking the elevator to move in

This is where most first-time condo renters get caught out. Downtown buildings are busy, and end-of-month weekends book up weeks ahead.

Move-in checklist

  • Ask management for the move-in form as soon as the lease is signed.
  • Reserve your elevator slot. Many buildings offer fixed windows and limit how long you can hold the elevator.
  • Pay any elevator deposit the building requires. It's charged by the corporation and is usually refundable if nothing is damaged. Ask in writing whether you or your landlord pays it.
  • Check mover requirements. Some buildings want a certificate of insurance from the moving company or restrict which entrance and loading dock you use.
  • Confirm loading dock and truck parking. Downtown streets near your building may have tight parking rules.
  • Register as a resident with concierge or the management office.
  • Book the move-out the same way when the time comes.

Fobs, keys and deposits

Most condos use electronic fobs for the front door, elevators, garage and amenities.

  • Count your fobs, keys, mailbox key and parking remote on move-in day and note them in writing.
  • In tenancies covered by the Residential Tenancies Act, a landlord can collect a refundable key deposit (no more than the expected replacement cost) and a last month's rent deposit, but not a separate damage deposit.
  • Replacement fobs ordered through the management office often carry a fee, so keep them safe.

Condo fees, utilities and insurance

  • Condo fees (common expenses) are paid by the owner to the corporation. They're normally built into your rent.
  • Utilities: some buildings include heat and water in the fees; many newer units have hydro (and sometimes water) metered separately. Ask exactly what you'll pay and to which company.
  • Tenant insurance: many condo leases require it, and it covers your belongings and liability. The owner's policy doesn't cover your things.

Short-term rentals in Toronto condos

Thinking of renting your place out while you travel? In Toronto, short-term rentals are allowed only in your principal residence, must be registered with the City, and the registration number must appear in every listing. For condos, the building's rules must permit it, and many condo corporations ban short-term rentals entirely. The City also recommends that tenants check their lease or get written consent from their landlord first. Breaking any of these can cause problems with the City, the corporation and your landlord.

This is general information, not legal advice. For questions about your tenancy, contact the Landlord and Tenant Board or a community legal clinic.

Frequently asked questions

Do condo rules apply to tenants in Ontario?

Yes. Under Ontario's Condominium Act, tenants and other residents must follow the condo's declaration, by-laws and rules, not just the owner. The owner also has to take reasonable steps to make sure their tenants comply, so a rule breach can cause trouble with both the corporation and your landlord.

How do I book the elevator to move into a Toronto condo?

Contact the management office or concierge as soon as your lease is signed and ask for the move-in form. Most buildings require a reserved time slot, padded elevator, and often a refundable deposit; some also ask for proof that you are a registered resident or a certificate of insurance from your movers. Weekend slots at the end of the month go fast.

Who pays condo fees when you rent a condo?

The owner. Condo fees (common expenses) are the owner's obligation to the condominium corporation, and they are usually built into the rent rather than charged to you separately. Your lease should say clearly which utilities, if any, you pay on top of rent.

Can I put my rented condo on Airbnb?

Usually not without several checks. In Toronto, short-term rentals are only allowed in your principal residence and must be registered with the City, the condo's rules must allow them, and your lease may forbid it. Many condo corporations ban short-term rentals outright.

Can a condo ban my pet even if my lease can't?

Possibly. In Ontario, a 'no pets' clause in a lease is generally void, but a condo's declaration can still restrict or prohibit pets, and courts have enforced those restrictions. Ask for the declaration's pet rules before you sign if you have or plan to get a pet.
