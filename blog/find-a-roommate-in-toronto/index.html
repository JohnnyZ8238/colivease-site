Find a Roommate in Toronto: The 2026 Guide

How to find a roommate in Toronto in 2026: where to look, how to vet people, red flags to avoid, what to ask when you meet and how to put it in writing.

Updated October 31, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

To find a roommate in Toronto, decide your budget and must-haves first, then search two or three channels at once (matching apps, school or work networks, and trusted community groups). Shortlist people whose habits match yours, meet them by video and in person, verify identity and the listing, and put your arrangement in writing before anyone pays or moves in.

That's the short version. The rest of this guide walks through each step with the checks that keep you safe and the questions that help you pick someone you will actually enjoy living with.

Key takeaways

  • Start early. Four to six weeks before move-in is a comfortable runway; late summer is the most competitive time in Toronto.
  • Match on habits, not just budget. Sleep schedule, cleanliness and guests cause more fights than money does.
  • Verify before you pay. Confirm the person, the unit and who actually has the right to rent it.
  • Know your legal setup. Co-tenant on the lease, roommate of a tenant, or renting from a live-in owner each give you different protections.
  • Write it down. A one- or two-page roommate agreement prevents most disputes.

Step 1: Get clear on what you need

Before you scroll a single listing, write down your answers to these. It makes every later conversation faster.

  • Budget: your maximum monthly rent plus your share of utilities and internet. Room prices in Toronto vary widely by neighbourhood, unit type and whether you get a private bathroom, so treat any number you hear as a rough guide.
  • Location: your commute to school or work, and how close you need to be to the TTC.
  • Move-in date and lease length: 4, 8 or 12 months? Flexible or fixed?
  • Must-haves: private bathroom, laundry in the unit, parking, pet-friendly.
  • Deal-breakers: smoking, overnight partners every night, a dog, a night-shift schedule.

If you are not sure which of these applies to a room you are considering, ask the person renting it directly, and check with the Landlord and Tenant Board or a legal clinic before you sign anything.

Step 2: Where to look for a roommate in Toronto

Use more than one channel. Each has strengths and weak spots.

  • Roommate-matching platforms. Good for filtering by budget, area and lifestyle before you start chatting. On ColivEase, for example, AI matching looks at habits, budget and schedule, and the "My Community" language filter helps you meet people who share your languages.
  • Your school or employer. University and college off-campus housing boards, alumni groups and workplace channels give you a built-in layer of accountability.
  • Friends and referrals. Still one of the safest routes. Ask your network early, even people you think are unlikely to know someone.
  • Community and social media groups. Neighbourhood, cultural and newcomer groups are busy and useful, but they are also where many fake listings appear.
  • General classifieds. Big reach, minimal screening. Expect to do all of the verification yourself.

Write a profile that attracts the right people

Be specific and honest. "Quiet nurse, works three 12-hour night shifts a week, tidy, no pets, occasional weekend guest" will get you better matches than "easygoing, clean, fun." Mention your budget range, target neighbourhoods and move-in date up front.

Step 3: Shortlist and screen

Once messages start coming in, screen quickly and politely.

First-message checklist

  • They answered your actual questions rather than sending a generic reply
  • Their budget and move-in date fit yours
  • They're willing to do a live video call
  • They didn't ask for money, ID scans or banking details at this stage

Red flags to walk away from

  • Asking for a deposit or "holding fee" before you've seen the place or signed anything
  • Refusing a live video call or in-person viewing with a vague excuse
  • A rent price far below everything comparable in the area
  • Pressure to decide today because "lots of people are interested"
  • Stories that change: a different unit, a different landlord, a different reason they're overseas
  • Requests for payment by gift card, crypto or wire transfer to an individual abroad

Step 4: The first meeting

Do a short video call first, then meet in person, ideally at the unit. Bring a list of questions. The big topics are money, cleaning, guests, noise, sleep schedules, work-from-home routines, food sharing and pets. We put together a ready-to-use list of 30 questions to ask a potential roommate.

A few tips:

  • Ask for examples, not labels. "What does clean mean to you? How often do you do dishes?" works better than "Are you clean?"
  • Share your own quirks. It invites honesty and shows you're serious.
  • Notice how they handle disagreement. If you differ on something small, do they listen or dismiss you?
  • Meet safely. Pick a public place or bring a friend if you're meeting at a private home for the first time, and tell someone where you're going.

Step 5: Verify the person and the place

This step separates a smooth move from a costly mistake.

Verify the person

  • Confirm their name matches their profile and any references.
  • Ask for a reference from a previous landlord or roommate and actually call it.
  • If they're a student or employee, a quick look at a school or work profile can confirm the basics.

Verify the unit and the person renting it

  • See the unit in person, or by live video if you truly can't travel, with the person walking room to room in real time.
  • If an agent is involved, check that they're registered with the Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO).
  • If you're renting directly, ask who owns the unit and how the person has the right to rent it out.
  • Make sure there will be a written lease. For most residential tenancies in Ontario, landlords must use the government's standard lease form.

Know the deposit rules

In most Ontario tenancies covered by the Residential Tenancies Act, a landlord can ask for a rent deposit of no more than one month's rent (used for the last month) and a refundable key deposit, but not a separate "damage deposit." Never pay anything before you've seen the place and have a signed lease in hand.

Step 6: Put it in writing

Once you've chosen someone, write a short roommate agreement. Cover:

  • How rent is split and who pays the landlord
  • How utilities and internet are divided and paid
  • Chores and shared supplies
  • Guests and overnight visitors
  • Quiet hours
  • What happens if someone wants to move out early

Step 7: Move in on the right foot

  • Do a move-in walkthrough together and take dated photos of every room.
  • Set up shared bills in a way that's easy to track.
  • Agree on a check-in. A quick chat after the first two weeks catches small annoyances before they grow.

Finding a roommate as a newcomer or student

If you're new to Canada, you may not have a credit history or local references yet. That's normal. Offer what you do have: a school acceptance letter, an employment letter, proof of funds or a reference from a previous landlord abroad.

Be extra careful about paying from overseas. Many scams target people who can't view a unit before arriving. If you can, book short-term accommodation for your first couple of weeks and view rooms in person.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the best place to find a roommate in Toronto?

Most people use a mix of channels: roommate-matching apps, university housing boards, community groups and referrals from friends or coworkers. Matching platforms save time because you can filter by budget, schedule and habits before you ever chat. Whatever channel you use, verify the person and the listing before paying anything.

How long does it take to find a roommate in Toronto?

It varies a lot with the season, your budget and how flexible you are on location. Give yourself at least four to six weeks before your move-in date, and more if you are searching during the busy late-summer student season.

Do I need a written roommate agreement in Ontario?

It is not legally required, but it is strongly recommended. A short written agreement covering rent, bills, chores, guests and moving out prevents most disputes. It is a private agreement between roommates and does not replace the lease you have with the landlord.

Does the Residential Tenancies Act protect me if I rent a room?

Often, but not always. In Ontario the Act generally does not apply if you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or certain close family members of the owner who live there. Check your situation with the Landlord and Tenant Board or a legal clinic before you sign.

Is it safe to find a roommate on social media or group chats?

It can be, but scams and fake profiles are common. Meet in person or by live video, confirm identity, never pay a deposit before seeing the place and signing a lease, and be wary of anyone who pressures you to decide fast.
