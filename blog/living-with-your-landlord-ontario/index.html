Living With Your Landlord in Ontario: Know the RTA Gap

Living with your landlord in Ontario? If you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner, the RTA may not apply. Learn what changes and how to protect yourself.

Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

If you rent a room in Ontario and must share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner (or the owner's spouse, child or parent) who lives in the same building, the Residential Tenancies Act (RTA) usually does not apply to you. That means no Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB), no standard lease requirement and no RTA rules on notice or deposits. Your protection comes mostly from what you agree to in writing, so a clear room rental agreement matters more than ever.

Key takeaways

  • The RTA exemption in section 5(i) applies when you must share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or certain close family members who live in the building.
  • If you have your own kitchen and bathroom (for example, a self-contained basement unit), the RTA generally still protects you, even if the owner lives upstairs.
  • In an exempt arrangement, the LTB can't hear your dispute. Money claims usually go to Small Claims Court.
  • Rules like the one-month deposit cap and the annual rent increase guideline don't automatically apply, so negotiate them into your agreement.
  • A written agreement covering notice, deposits, house rules and guests is your best protection.

When the RTA doesn't apply: the shared kitchen or bathroom test

Section 5(i) of the Residential Tenancies Act, 2006 exempts living accommodation where the occupant is required to share a bathroom or kitchen with:

  • the owner,
  • the owner's spouse,
  • the owner's child or parent, or
  • the spouse's child or parent,

and that person lives in the building where the room is.

Both parts matter. Sharing a kitchen with other renters (but not the owner) does not trigger this exemption. And if the owner's family member only drops by, but doesn't live there, the test isn't met.

What changes when you live with the owner

Living with your landlord can be a great setup: often furnished, utilities included, and sometimes cheaper than a full apartment. But you should know what you're giving up.

Notice to leave

Under the RTA, landlords must use specific LTB notices and can only end a tenancy for reasons the law allows. In an exempt arrangement, those rules don't apply. There's no N12 form, no LTB hearing and no eviction order needed. The owner can generally ask you to leave on whatever notice your agreement sets out, or on "reasonable notice" if your agreement is silent.

That's why a written notice period (for example, 30 days for either side) is so important. It gives both of you predictability.

Deposits

Under the RTA, a landlord can only collect a rent deposit of no more than one month's rent (plus a refundable key deposit), and damage deposits aren't allowed. In an exempt arrangement, a live-in owner can ask for a damage deposit or a different amount. If you pay one:

  • get the amount and purpose in writing,
  • write down when and how it will be returned,
  • list what deductions are allowed (for example, unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear),
  • pay by e-transfer or cheque, never cash without a receipt.

For how deposits work under the RTA, see our guide to rent deposit rules in Ontario.

Rent increases

The provincial rent increase guideline and the 12-month rule come from the RTA. If you're exempt, your agreement controls how and when rent can go up. Ask for a clause that says rent stays the same for a set period and that any increase needs written notice.

Privacy and entry

The RTA's 24-hour written notice before entry doesn't apply. Since you live in the owner's home, shared spaces are shared. But your bedroom is still your space, so put entry rules in writing too (for example, the owner only enters your room with permission or in an emergency).

Human rights protections are narrower too

The Ontario Human Rights Code has a similar exemption. Under section 21(1), the Code's housing protections don't apply when the occupant must share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's family who live there. In plain terms, a live-in owner has more freedom to choose who they live with. That's another reason to clarify expectations early.

How to protect yourself with a written agreement

A room rental agreement doesn't need to be complicated. It should be clear, signed by both of you, and each person should keep a copy.

Checklist: what to include

  • Names of both parties and the full address, including which room is yours
  • Start date, and whether it's month-to-month or a fixed term
  • Monthly rent, the due date and how you'll pay
  • What's included: utilities, internet, laundry, furniture, parking
  • Deposit amount, what it covers and when it comes back
  • Notice period to end the arrangement, for both sides
  • Shared spaces you can use (kitchen, living room, fridge and pantry space)
  • House rules: quiet hours, guests and overnight guests, cleaning, smoking, pets
  • When the owner can enter your bedroom
  • How rent increases work, if any
  • How disputes will be handled (for example, a talk first, then written notice)

If you're signing a roommate-style agreement with other renters, our roommate agreement guide has more clauses you can borrow.

Keep a paper trail

  • Take dated photos of your room on move-in day.
  • Keep receipts or e-transfer records for every rent and deposit payment.
  • Confirm important conversations by text or email ("Just confirming we agreed I'll move out on March 31").

Before you say yes: questions to ask the owner

Living with someone is personal. Ask these before you commit:

  1. Who else lives here, and who will I share the kitchen or bathroom with?
  2. Are there cooking times, food storage rules or kitchen limits?
  3. What are the expectations around guests, partners and overnight visitors?
  4. What happens if either of us wants to end the arrangement?
  5. Is the deposit refundable, and what would you deduct for?
  6. Is there a written agreement I can review before paying anything?

A good host won't mind these questions. If someone refuses to put anything in writing or pressures you to send money before a viewing, slow down. Those are classic warning signs of a rental scam.

If something goes wrong

Because the LTB usually can't help in an exempt arrangement, your options look different:

  • Talk first. Many problems (noise, cleaning, kitchen use) are solved with a direct conversation and a written follow-up.
  • Small Claims Court. For money disputes, like a deposit that isn't returned or rent you prepaid, Ontario's Small Claims Court handles claims up to $50,000.
  • Police. If you feel unsafe, or someone is threatening you or holding your belongings, call the police. Call 911 in an emergency.
  • Legal help. A community legal clinic can tell you whether the RTA really doesn't apply. Sometimes an arrangement that looks exempt isn't (for example, if the owner doesn't actually live there).

Frequently asked questions

Does the Residential Tenancies Act apply if I live with my landlord in Ontario?

Usually not, if you have to share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's spouse, child or parent (or the spouse's child or parent) and that person lives in the same building. This exemption is in section 5(i) of the RTA. If you have your own kitchen and bathroom, the RTA generally still applies even if the owner lives upstairs.

How much notice does a live-in landlord have to give me in Ontario?

Because the RTA's notice rules don't apply in an exempt arrangement, there is no LTB notice form or fixed statutory period. Your written agreement and general contract law decide what is fair, which is why it's smart to put a notice period, such as 30 days, in writing before you move in.

Can I take a live-in landlord to the Landlord and Tenant Board?

Generally no. If the RTA doesn't apply, the LTB has no jurisdiction over the arrangement. Money disputes, like an unreturned deposit, usually go to Small Claims Court, which handles claims up to $50,000 in Ontario.

Can a live-in landlord ask for a damage deposit?

The RTA's limit of one month's rent as a deposit doesn't apply to exempt arrangements, so a live-in owner can ask for a damage deposit. Get the amount, the conditions for keeping any of it, and the return date in writing, and pay in a traceable way.

Is the arrangement covered if I share a kitchen with the landlord's adult son but the owner lives elsewhere?

The exemption covers the owner's child if that child lives in the building and you must share the kitchen or bathroom with them. Situations like this can be fact-specific, so contact the LTB or a community legal clinic if you're not sure.
