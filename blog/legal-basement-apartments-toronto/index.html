Legal Basement Apartment Toronto: What to Check First

Renting a legal basement apartment in Toronto? Check exit windows, smoke and CO alarms, ceiling height and heat, and know the rights you keep if it isn't legal.

Updated October 31, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

A legal basement apartment in Toronto is a second suite that's allowed by the City's zoning and built to meet the Ontario Building Code and Fire Code, usually with a building permit. Before you rent, check for a safe second way out, working smoke and carbon monoxide (CO) alarms, enough ceiling height and reliable heat. Even if a basement unit isn't fully legal, you're still protected by the Residential Tenancies Act (RTA) in most cases.

Key takeaways

  • Toronto zoning allows a second suite in most houses, but legal also means built to code with permits.
  • Safety first: a second exit or large enough window, interconnected smoke alarms, and CO alarms where there's fuel-burning equipment.
  • Your RTA rights generally still apply in a basement unit, legal or not, unless you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner who lives there.
  • A landlord can't lock you out because the unit isn't legal. Evictions go through the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB).
  • Report fire-safety problems to your landlord in writing, then to 311 if they aren't fixed.

This is general information, not legal advice. For your situation, contact the Landlord and Tenant Board or a community legal clinic.

What "legal" means for a Toronto basement apartment

There are really two layers:

  1. Zoning: is a second suite allowed here? The City of Toronto's zoning bylaw allows a secondary suite in most detached houses, semi-detached houses and townhouses across the city.
  2. Building and fire safety: was it built properly? The suite needs to meet the Ontario Building Code and the Ontario Fire Code. Creating a new suite generally requires a building permit and inspections.

Plenty of basement units in Toronto were finished years ago without permits. They may be on a street where suites are allowed but still miss key safety features. That's why it's worth checking the unit itself, not just asking "is this legal?"

Basement apartment checklist: what to look at during a viewing

Bring this list and your phone's flashlight. You don't need to be an inspector to spot the big issues.

Getting out safely

  • Is there a separate entrance, or a clear exit that doesn't go through the main unit?
  • If there's only one door, is there a bedroom window big enough to climb out of? Building Code standards for exit windows commonly call for an opening of at least 0.35 m², with no side narrower than 380 mm. Ask the landlord to confirm it meets code.
  • Can the window actually be opened from inside without tools? Is it blocked by bars, furniture or a deep window well?
  • Is the path to the exit free of storage and clutter?

Alarms

  • Is there a working smoke alarm in the unit and outside the sleeping area? Ask the landlord to press the test button.
  • In a house with two units, are the smoke alarms interconnected, so an alarm upstairs sounds downstairs too?
  • Is there a carbon monoxide alarm near the bedrooms? Ontario requires CO alarms in homes with a fuel-burning appliance (such as a gas furnace or water heater), a fireplace or an attached garage.

Space and comfort

  • Ceiling height: can you stand comfortably everywhere? Secondary suites are allowed somewhat lower ceilings than a typical new home, roughly 1.95 m in main areas and a bit less under beams and ducts, but the exact rules depend on when and how the suite was built.
  • Heat: does the unit have its own heat source or vents, and do you control the thermostat? Ask how warm it stays in January.
  • Moisture: any musty smell, water stains, peeling paint or white crust on the walls? Check under sinks and in corners.
  • Light and air: are there windows that open? Is there a bathroom fan that vents outside?
  • Furnace room: is it separated from the living space with a door, rather than open to your bedroom?
  • Electrical: are there enough outlets, or would you need extension cords everywhere?

Paperwork and practical details

  • Ask whether the suite was built with a building permit and passed inspection.
  • Ask who pays utilities and whether your hydro is separate or shared with upstairs.
  • Ask whether laundry is shared and when you can use it.
  • Will you get the Ontario Standard Lease? Most residential tenancies in Ontario need one. See our guide to the Ontario standard lease.

Questions to ask the landlord

  1. Was this suite built with a building permit, and did it pass final inspection?
  2. When were the smoke and CO alarms last tested or replaced?
  3. Has the basement ever flooded or had water come in? What was done about it?
  4. How is the unit heated, and is there air conditioning or a dehumidifier in summer?
  5. Who lives upstairs, and do we share laundry, the entrance or the backyard?
  6. Are utilities included, or split with the upstairs unit? How is the split calculated?

The biggest practical risk with an unpermitted unit isn't your legal status. It's safety, and the chance that the City orders work or the unit stops being rented, forcing you to move on short notice.

Your rights still apply in a basement unit

If you have your own kitchen and bathroom, the RTA generally covers you in a basement apartment, permitted or not. That means:

  • your rent deposit can't be more than one month's rent,
  • rent increases follow the provincial rules,
  • the landlord must keep the unit in a good state of repair and meet health, safety and maintenance standards,
  • the landlord must give proper written notice before entering, except in emergencies, and
  • the landlord needs a valid notice and an LTB order to evict you if you don't leave voluntarily.

One big exception: if you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner (or the owner's close family) who lives in the house, the RTA may not apply. Read our guide to living with your landlord in Ontario.

Also, don't stop paying rent because the unit has problems. Withholding rent can lead to an eviction application against you. Use the proper process instead.

How to report a problem

  1. Tell your landlord in writing. Email or text works. Describe the problem, add photos and ask for a repair date.
  2. For smoke or CO alarm problems, the City of Toronto advises tenants to notify the landlord, and if the landlord doesn't act, to call 311 for a referral to Toronto Fire Services.
  3. For other maintenance or property standards issues (heat, mould, pests, leaks), you can also contact 311.
  4. Apply to the LTB. If repairs aren't done, a tenant can file a maintenance application (Form T6). If the landlord harasses you or locks you out, Form T2 covers tenant rights issues.
  5. Emergencies: if there's fire, smoke or a CO alarm going off, get out and call 911.

Frequently asked questions

Are basement apartments legal in Toronto?

Yes, the City of Toronto's zoning allows second suites in most detached houses, semi-detached houses and townhouses. A suite also needs to meet the Ontario Building Code and Fire Code, and creating one generally requires a building permit. A basement can be allowed by zoning but still not be built to code.

Do I have tenant rights if my basement apartment is illegal?

In most cases, yes. The Residential Tenancies Act generally applies to a rental unit whether or not the suite was built with permits, unless an exemption applies, such as sharing a kitchen or bathroom with the owner who lives there. Your landlord still needs to follow the LTB process to end your tenancy.

How do I know if a basement apartment is legal?

Ask the landlord whether the suite was created with a building permit and passed inspection, and ask to see the paperwork. Also check the basics yourself: a second way out, working interconnected smoke alarms, CO alarms if there's a furnace or other fuel-burning appliance, and adequate heat and ceiling height.

Who do I call about fire safety problems in my Toronto basement unit?

Tell your landlord in writing first. If smoke or CO alarms aren't fixed, call 311, which can refer the issue to Toronto Fire Services for inspection. Call 911 if there's smoke, fire or a CO alarm sounding.

Can my landlord evict me because the basement isn't legal?

Your landlord can't simply change the locks or tell you to leave. To end a tenancy covered by the RTA, they need a valid notice and, if you don't leave, an order from the Landlord and Tenant Board. Get advice quickly if this happens.
