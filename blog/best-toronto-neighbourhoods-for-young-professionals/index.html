Best Neighbourhoods to Rent in Toronto for Professionals

The best neighbourhoods to rent in Toronto for young professionals: King West, Liberty Village, Yonge & Eglinton, North York and more, compared.

Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

The best neighbourhoods to rent in Toronto as a young professional are the ones that match your commute and lifestyle: King West and the downtown core if you want to walk to the office and go out often, Liberty Village for a condo community with a short trip downtown, Yonge & Eglinton or North York Centre for a direct subway ride and more space for the money, and Leslieville, the Annex or the Danforth for a real neighbourhood feel. Each one trades off price, space, noise and commute differently, so decide which trade-off you can live with first.

Key takeaways

  • Commute shapes everything. A direct subway ride is usually more predictable than a streetcar, and a walk is better than both.
  • Downtown and King West offer the shortest commutes and best nightlife, at the highest rents and smallest units.
  • Midtown and North York along Line 1 give you more space for the money and a straight shot to the Financial District.
  • East and west-end neighbourhoods like Leslieville, the Danforth and the Annex feel more residential but rely on streetcars or a transfer.
  • Newer condos aren't rent-controlled. Units first occupied after November 15, 2018 are generally exempt from Ontario's rent increase guideline.

How to compare neighbourhoods

Before you fall for a balcony view, run every option through four questions:

  1. What is the door-to-door commute at 8:30 a.m.? Include walking, waiting and transfers. Streetcars share the road with traffic, so their travel times vary more than the subway's.
  2. How often will you actually go downtown? If you're hybrid and in the office two or three days a week, a slightly longer commute in exchange for a second bedroom or home office can be a great deal.
  3. What does the all-in cost look like? Condo rentals often bundle some utilities; house apartments may not. Ask what's included.
  4. What will you do on a Tuesday night? Gym, park, groceries and a decent coffee within walking distance matter more over a year than a rooftop pool you use twice.

The neighbourhoods up close

Downtown core and St. Lawrence

If your office is near King and Bay, living downtown means walking to work, skipping transit delays and being close to everything from concerts to the waterfront. St. Lawrence, around the market, is a bit calmer than the towers near Union Station. Expect high rents for compact condo units and busy streets. This is a strong choice if you work long or unpredictable hours.

King West

King West is restaurant-and-bar central, with converted warehouses and newer condos. The King streetcar runs along the street, but many people simply walk to the core. It's ideal if you're out several nights a week and don't mind noise on weekends. If you're a light sleeper, look at units facing away from King Street.

Liberty Village

Liberty Village packs condos, gyms, grocery stores and coffee shops into a compact former industrial area. It's very popular with young professionals and dog owners. The catch is getting out: the King streetcar fills up at rush hour, and there are only a few streets in and out. Exhibition GO station on the Lakeshore West line is a handy alternative, one stop from Union.

The Annex

The Annex mixes Victorian houses split into apartments, a few low-rise buildings and plenty of character. Bloor Street has bookstores, cafés and cinemas, and you're close to both Line 1 and Line 2. You'll trade modern amenities like in-suite laundry and gyms for charm, and you'll be living near the U of T crowd, which some love and some don't.

Yonge & Eglinton

Midtown's busiest node has lots of newer condos, restaurants and shopping. Line 1 runs straight downtown, and Eglinton station is also an interchange with Line 5 Eglinton, which makes cross-town trips easier. It has a younger, professional feel and is generally a little cheaper than the core for similar space, though not cheap.

North York Centre

Along Yonge between Sheppard and Finch, North York Centre offers taller buildings, larger units for the money and a straight Line 1 ride downtown. Line 4 Sheppard connects at Sheppard-Yonge. You'll find a big mix of Korean, Chinese and Persian restaurants along Yonge, and quieter evenings than downtown. Great if you value space and calm, less great if your social life is mostly in the core.

Leslieville

East of the Don River, Leslieville is known for brunch spots, independent shops, parks and a strong neighbourhood identity. You'll find a mix of house apartments and newer mid-rise condos. Commutes rely on the Queen and King streetcars, and major transit construction for the Ontario Line is underway in parts of the area, which can mean noise and detours for now.

The Danforth

Greektown and the surrounding Danforth neighbourhoods have patios, grocers and many houses split into upper and lower flats. Line 2 runs right under Danforth Avenue; to reach the Financial District, you'll transfer to Line 1 at Bloor-Yonge. It feels residential while staying well connected.

Also worth a look

Roncesvalles and High Park on the west side offer a park-heavy, village feel on Line 2 and the King and Queen streetcars. Davisville, just south of Yonge & Eglinton, is a little quieter than its neighbour with similar transit access.

Rent control, renewals and budgeting

Before signing, ask when the unit was first occupied. Ontario's rent increase guideline for 2026 is 2.1%, but most units first occupied after November 15, 2018, which includes many newer condos, are exempt from it. That means your rent could rise more at renewal. Our guide to the 2026 rent increase guideline explains the details.

For budgeting, CMHC's benchmark is that housing is affordable when it costs less than 30% of before-tax income. In Toronto that's hard to hit alone: TRREB's rental report for the second quarter of 2026 put the average one-bedroom condo apartment leased across the GTA at about $2,270 a month. For a full breakdown of what renting costs, see our guide to the cost of renting a room in Toronto.

Sharing as a professional

Plenty of young professionals in Toronto share a two-bedroom condo or a house rather than paying for a one-bedroom alone. It can free up money for savings or travel, and it often gets you a better location than you could afford solo. The key is finding someone whose schedule and habits fit yours. Frequently asked questions

What is the best area in Toronto for young professionals?

It depends on your priorities. King West and the downtown core suit people who want to walk to work and go out often, Yonge & Eglinton and North York Centre suit people who want more space and a direct subway ride, and Leslieville or the Annex suit people who want a neighbourhood feel close to downtown.

Is Liberty Village a good place to live?

Liberty Village is popular with young professionals for its newer condos, restaurants, gyms and dog-friendly streets. The main drawbacks are crowded King streetcars at rush hour and limited ways in and out. Exhibition GO station on the Lakeshore West line is an alternative to the streetcar for getting downtown.

Is it worth living in North York and commuting downtown?

For many renters, yes. Line 1 runs directly from North York Centre and Sheppard-Yonge to the Financial District, typically in about half an hour, and you often get more space or a newer building for the money. It is less ideal if you go out downtown most evenings.

How much of my income should go to rent in Toronto?

CMHC considers housing affordable when it costs less than 30% of a household's before-tax income, and that is a useful starting point for a budget. In Toronto many renters spend more, which is why sharing a larger unit with a roommate is common among young professionals.

Are newer condo rentals in Toronto covered by rent control?

In Ontario, most units first occupied for residential purposes after November 15, 2018 are exempt from the annual rent increase guideline. Older units are generally covered. Ask when the unit was first occupied before you sign, because it affects how much your rent can rise each year.
