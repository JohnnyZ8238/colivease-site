Co-Living vs Renting Alone in Toronto: Which Is Better?

Co-living or a shared rental vs renting alone in Toronto: costs from CMHC data, privacy, flexibility, who is on the lease, who each suits and making it work.

Updated November 3, 2026 · 8 min read · ColivEase

For most people in Toronto, co-living or a shared rental is cheaper than renting alone. CMHC's October 2025 averages put a one-bedroom at $1,761 a month, while half of an average two-bedroom is about $1,023. Renting alone buys privacy, control and simpler legal arrangements. Sharing trades some of that for lower costs and company, and works best when you choose roommates carefully and know who is on the lease.

This guide compares the two on cost, lifestyle, privacy, flexibility and legal protection, and explains who each option suits.

Key takeaways

  • Sharing usually saves money, often several hundred dollars a month compared with a one-bedroom.
  • New leases cost more than averages. CMHC found new tenants pay more than tenants who've stayed put.
  • Privacy and control are the main reasons to rent alone.
  • Legal protection depends on the setup, especially whether you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner.
  • Shared living works when expectations match and are written down.

What counts as co-living?

People use "co-living" for a few different setups:

  • Co-tenants: two or more people sign one lease for a whole unit.
  • Renting a room from a tenant: one person holds the lease and rents rooms to others.
  • Room-by-room rentals: a landlord or operator rents each bedroom separately, often furnished, with shared common areas. In Toronto, a home where four or more rooms are rented to separate people is a multi-tenant (rooming) house, and the City requires operators to be licensed.
  • Living with the owner: you rent a room in the owner's home and share their kitchen or bathroom.

Each one comes with different rights, which we cover below.

Cost: what the data says

Average rents

Based on these averages:

  • Renting alone: about $1,490 for a studio or $1,760 for a one-bedroom.
  • Splitting a two-bedroom: about $1,020 to $1,030 each.
  • Renting a room: around $1,150 on the City's figure.

Why your quote may be higher

These averages include tenants who have lived in their units for years. CMHC's 2025 Rental Market Report found that, on average, new tenants were paying higher rents than sitting tenants. In Toronto, the average rent for a two-bedroom purpose-built unit that changed tenants was $2,547 in October 2025, down from $2,612 in 2024. Even at that level, two people sharing would pay about $1,274 each, still less than the average one-bedroom.

CMHC also noted more competition from rented condo apartments in Toronto as owners shifted units into the rental market, which gives renters more choice in some areas.

Lifestyle and privacy

Renting alone

  • Quiet, your own schedule and your own rules
  • Easier for working from home, night shifts, and hosting a partner
  • No negotiating over chores, guests or the thermostat
  • Can feel isolating, especially for newcomers

Sharing

  • Built-in company and someone to split chores with
  • Bigger or better-located homes become affordable
  • Less privacy and more compromise: bathroom schedules, kitchen space, noise
  • Your experience depends heavily on who you live with

Flexibility

  • Renting alone: you control the lease. To leave, you normally give your landlord 60 days' notice on Form N9, timed to the end of a rental period or the lease term.
  • Co-tenants: leaving early is harder, because one person's notice can affect everyone on the lease. You'll usually need to agree on a replacement, with the landlord's consent.
  • Renting a room from a tenant, or from the owner: notice depends on your agreement, which can make moving in and out faster, but also gives you less security.
  • Room-by-room rentals: each room is often a separate agreement, so a roommate leaving doesn't change your rent.

Legal differences: who's on the lease

Renting from another tenantThe tenantOften noYour agreement; Small Claims Court for money

Key points:

  • The LTB's guide to the Act says a rental unit can be an apartment, a house, or a room in a rooming or boarding house, so renting a single room from a landlord who doesn't live there is usually covered.
  • Most Ontario tenancies use the standard lease, which says, among other things, that terms banning guests or roommates are void and that assignment or subletting needs the landlord's consent.
  • The Residential Tenancies Act generally doesn't apply if you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or their close family living there. Steps to Justice also notes it might not apply when you share housing with roommates. See living with your landlord in Ontario.
  • Co-tenants are generally each responsible to the landlord for the full rent, so choose people you trust with money.

Who each option suits

Renting alone suits you if:

  • You work from home most days or work nights
  • You value quiet and routine above savings
  • Your income comfortably covers a full unit
  • You're in a stable job and plan to stay a while

Sharing or co-living suits you if:

  • You're a student, newcomer or early in your career
  • You want a better location than you could afford alone
  • You like company and don't mind compromise
  • You want furnished, flexible options while you get settled

How to make shared living work

  1. Choose roommates on habits, not just availability. Talk about sleep, cleanliness, guests, money and noise before anyone signs.
  2. Know your setup before you sign: co-tenant, roommate of a tenant, room-by-room lease, or living with the owner.
  3. Write a roommate agreement covering rent, bills, chores, guests, quiet hours and moving out.
  4. Split costs fairly. Bigger rooms or private bathrooms can pay more; see how to split rent fairly.
  5. Keep records of every payment.
  6. Check in monthly and fix small issues early.

Bottom line

Renting alone costs more but gives you privacy, control and usually full protection under the Residential Tenancies Act. Sharing saves real money, often several hundred dollars a month, and can be a great way to settle into Toronto, as long as you pick compatible people, understand who's on the lease, and put your house rules in writing.

This article is general information, not legal advice. For your specific situation, contact the Landlord and Tenant Board or a community legal clinic.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to live with roommates in Toronto?

Usually, yes. CMHC's October 2025 survey, as summarized by U of T Student Life, put the average Toronto rent at $1,761 for a one-bedroom and $2,045 for a two-bedroom, so splitting a two-bedroom costs about $1,023 each. Rents for new leases are usually higher than these averages, but the gap between sharing and living alone generally remains.

What is co-living?

Co-living usually means renting a private bedroom in a shared home, with the kitchen, living room and sometimes bathrooms shared. Some homes are run by an operator who rents each room separately, often furnished; others are simply roommates sharing a regular rental. The legal setup varies, so always check who you're signing with and what you're signing.

Does the Residential Tenancies Act apply to shared housing?

Sometimes. It generally doesn't apply if you share a kitchen or bathroom with the owner or the owner's close family who live there. Steps to Justice also notes it might not apply when you share with roommates, for example if you rent from another tenant. If you sign a lease directly with a landlord who doesn't live there, it usually does.

Who should rent alone in Toronto?

Renting alone suits people who need quiet and control, such as those working night shifts, working from home most days, or with strong routines, and whose budget can absorb a full unit's rent. CMHC treats housing as affordable when it costs under 30% of before-tax income, a useful check before committing.

How do I make living with roommates work?

Choose roommates with similar habits and budgets, agree on house rules before moving in, and write them down in a roommate agreement. Cover rent shares, bills, chores, guests, quiet hours and moving out. Keep payment records, hold a short house meeting each month, and raise small issues early.
